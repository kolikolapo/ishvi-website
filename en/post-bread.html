11th Century - Beer during fasting.

February 20, 2026

In medieval Europe, fasting for monks was not just a spiritual practice; it was also a physical test. During long periods of fasting, solid food was strictly limited, yet the body still needed energy.

This is where beer emerges as a means of survival. For monks, beer was "liquid bread"—nutritious, caloric, and safe. The malt and grains it contained gave the body strength, while the fermentation process made the drink stable and reliable.

Liquid Bread and the Birth of Doppelbock

Monks were not just brewers; they were the scientists of their time. They noticed that the more malt they used, the more nourishing the drink became. In the monastic breweries of the 11th century, the ancestor of the so-called "Doppelbock" was brewed—a dark, viscous, and sweet liquid so saturated with vitamins and carbohydrates that a single mug could replenish a whole day's energy supply.

In many monasteries, there was a special bell that called the monks to drink beer. Because eating food in the morning was forbidden during strict fasting, the first mug of beer became their "liquid breakfast." This gave them the strength to go straight from morning prayers to work in the fields.

This was not drinking for pleasure; it was a necessity, calculated for survival and the continuation of labor.
